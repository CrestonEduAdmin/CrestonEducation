The case for sitting it

Why put yourself through another examination

Most doctors who reach the end of anaesthesia training have had enough of exams. The argument for this one is not that it makes you a better anaesthesiologist, though it will. It is that it is the credential you can start on now, from where you already are, that keeps the most doors open later.

You can start from where you are

Part I asks for a primary medical qualification and anaesthesia training. Not a European post, not prior European experience, not a relocation you have not yet made. Most international credentials require you to already be inside the system before you can begin. This one does not, and that is the whole point of it.

It travels between systems

It was built as a pan-European standard rather than one country's fellowship, so it is designed to be read the same way in several health systems at once. If you do not yet know which country you will end up in, that portability is worth more than a credential tied to a single one.

It removes examinations later

It is currently accepted as exemption from the Primary FRCA and from the Irish college's primary-level examination. Sitting one diploma can take an entire stage out of two other pathways — a saving in fees, travel and, more than either, in years.

There is no deadline on it

Part I can be sat during residency, and a pass does not expire. You can take it while the basic sciences are still fresh and cash it in five years later, which makes it one of the few career decisions here with almost no downside risk.

It makes your CV legible

A licensing assessor or a recruiter who cannot judge the standard of your national degree can judge this one immediately. That matters in Gulf licensing files, in European applications, and in non-clinical hiring where nobody on the panel is an anaesthesiologist.

It raises your clinical ceiling

The honest version: preparing for it drags your physiology, pharmacology and measurement back up to a level most of us quietly let slide after residency. Candidates routinely say the exam changed how they run a list long before it changed where they work.
