What you are actually sitting

Two parts, two completely different skills

Part I rewards precision under a format most doctors have never trained for. Part II rewards spoken structure under time pressure. People who prepare for both the same way tend to pass one and fail the other.

Format
Two papers, 60 stems each. Every stem carries five independent true/false statements.
Paper A
Anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, pharmacology, physics and clinical measurement.
Paper B
Clinical anaesthesia across subspecialties, regional, pain, intensive care, internal and emergency medicine.
Timing
120 minutes per paper on paper, 90 minutes if computer-based, with a break between the two.
Marking
One mark per statement, so 300 per paper. No negative marking — a blank scores the same as a wrong answer.
Passing
Both papers must be passed. The cut-off is set each year against the standard of the paper, so it is not a fixed percentage.
Language
Offered in several languages, held annually in September.

Why the format decides the result

Because each statement is marked independently, a stem you half-know is worth three or four marks, not zero. Because nothing is deducted for a wrong answer, every blank you leave is a mark you have donated back.

That changes how you should revise. You are not building recall of topics — you are building the ability to take any sentence about a topic and decide whether it is defensible. Creston's whole method is drilled around that conversion — turning what you read into testable true/false statements, then justifying each call out loud.

Format
Four Structured Oral Examinations in a single day — two basic science, two clinical.
Each station
25 minutes with two examiners, so eight examiners across the day.
Preparation
A short written brief is given roughly ten minutes before you meet the examiners.
Prerequisite
A Part I pass, which does not expire, plus specialist certification recognised for your country.

What separates a pass from a near miss

Candidates rarely fail Part II on knowledge. They fail on shape — answering the question that was asked, opening with a framework instead of a fact, and holding a position when an examiner pushes back without either collapsing or digging in.

That can only be trained by being examined, repeatedly, by someone who will tell you plainly what went wrong. Mock vivas with structured feedback are the core of the Part II programme.

Part I is open early. You need to hold a primary medical qualification and to be in or through anaesthesia training — you do not need European experience, and you do not need to be near the end of your residency. A pass never expires, so sitting it early costs you nothing later.

Part II is the end-of-training assessment and requires specialist certification that ESAIC recognises for your country. ESAIC publishes a country-by-country list of which national diplomas qualify.

Holders of the FRCA, FCAI or FCARCSI can be exempted from Part I and enter at Part II.

Check before you plan around it

Eligibility rules, fees and recognised diplomas are set by ESAIC and change between cycles. Everything on this site is a guide to help you think, not a substitute for the current official documents.

Always confirm against the ESAIC diploma guide and the Part II eligibility list for your country before you commit money or a year of preparation. If you want help reading what applies to you, that is a free conversation — just ask.
